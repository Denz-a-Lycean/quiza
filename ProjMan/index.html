📗 Lesson 1 — Introduction to Project Management

Course & Core Definitions

Course
Project Management (OPMN03C) introduces the discipline, tools, and mindset needed to initiate, plan, execute, monitor, control, and close projects effectively. It is ultimately about turning ideas into value.
Project
A temporary undertaking created to produce a unique product, service, system, or result. Examples include a mobile app, a campus event, a cybersecurity project, or a digital payment platform.
Project Management
The organized process of planning, organizing, executing, monitoring, and completing work to meet project goals. It keeps effort aligned with scope, time, resources, quality, and stakeholder expectations.
Key idea
A project is not just a list of tasks. It is a controlled effort designed to achieve a purpose under constraints and uncertainty.

The DNA of a project — T.U.G.R.C.

Temporary
Has a defined beginning and end.
Unique
Creates a specific deliverable that is unlike routine operations.
Goal-Oriented
Drives toward a clear objective or result.
Resource-Constrained
Exists within limits of budget, people, time, tools, and technology.
Coordinated
Requires people, tasks, timing, and communication to be aligned.
The Illusion vs. The Reality
Illusion: a good idea alone guarantees success. Reality: unclear requirements, unrealistic deadlines, limited resources, poor communication, unmanaged risks, and uncontrolled changes can derail progress. The role of PM is to create order in a changing environment.
Old Paradigm (Output-Driven)
Success was measured mainly by scope, schedule, and budget. This view is incomplete because it ignores whether the output actually produced value for the user and the organization.
Modern Paradigm (Outcome-Driven)
Vision → Deliverable → Benefit → Value/Outcomes. The project should not stop at producing a system or product; it should create outcomes that matter to stakeholders and generate organizational value. “Projects do not simply produce outputs; they enable those outputs to drive outcomes that ultimately deliver value.” — PMBOK® Guide, 7th Ed.
Project vs. Operation
Project: temporary and unique. Operation: ongoing and repetitive, focused on maintaining existing processes.
Output vs. Outcome vs. Value
Output is what the project produces; outcome is what changes because of it; value is the benefit or improvement gained.

Project Team Roles

Project Manager Responsibilities
Define objectives · Plan the project · Organize the team · Manage time · Manage resources · Manage risks · Communicate and coordinate stakeholder expectations.
Project Manager
The integrator and leader of the project. The PM aligns people, tasks, resources, time, quality, risk, and stakeholder communication around a shared goal.
Business Analyst
Gathers, analyzes, and documents stakeholder and system requirements so the project solves the right problem.
UI/UX Designer
Designs interfaces and experiences that are usable, practical, and aligned with user needs.
Programmer
Builds and maintains the system software based on approved requirements and architecture.
Database Developer
Designs the data model, structures, and database systems that support the application.
QA / Tester
Validates whether the system meets quality requirements and whether defects are detected and resolved before delivery.
Role reminder
A PM is not the “soloist” who does every task. The PM is the conductor who keeps the team coordinated, focused, and accountable.

The Anatomy of a Project Environment

Big picture
A project does not exist in isolation. It is influenced by business goals, organizational culture, team behavior, technical realities, and external uncertainty.
1 · Value System (Macro)
How the project supports broader organizational strategy, mission, and business value.
2 · The 12 Principles (Philosophy)
The behavioral compass and ethical foundation for project work.
3 · The 8 Domains (Execution)
The active engine of project performance, made up of interconnected activities that happen together.
4 · Tailoring & Frameworks (Micro)
Choosing and adapting methods such as Agile or Waterfall to fit the project’s context.

12 Principles — 4 Quadrants

Mindset & Stewardship
Be a responsible steward · Focus on value · Apply systems thinking.
People & Culture
Create collaborative teams · Engage stakeholders · Demonstrate leadership.
Navigation & Resilience
Navigate complexity · Optimize risk responses · Embrace adaptability · Enable change.
Execution & Craft
Build quality into processes · Tailor delivery based on context.

8 Project Performance Domains

Stakeholders · Team · Development Approach · Planning · Project Work · Delivery · Measurement · Uncertainty. These domains operate together rather than in a rigid sequence, which is why strong PM requires constant adjustment.

8 Guiding Questions

Problem · Goal · Scope · Ownership · Timeline · Resources · Risk · Success. These questions help establish a project’s direction before work begins.

The Five Building Blocks

1 · Objectives
SMART goals: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.
2 · Scope
Defines included work and excluded work. Strong scope control prevents scope creep, the uncontrolled addition of requirements beyond agreed boundaries.
3 · Deliverables
Tangible outputs such as a proposal, requirements document, prototype, test result, user guide, or final project report.
4 · Stakeholders
Anyone affected by or influencing the project, including the client, sponsor, team, managers, users, and administrators.
5 · Constraints
Scope, time, cost, quality, resources, and risk are interdependent. Change one and you likely change the others.
Remember
The project triangle is a simplification: time, cost, and scope are always connected. Quality and risk also influence the balance.

Mechanics, Life Cycle & Roles

Mechanics of PM
Start → Plan → Execute → Monitor → Complete. This flow is simple but powerful: define what matters, decide how to do it, carry it out, check progress, and close formally.

The Project Life Cycle — 5 phases

1 · Initiation
Clarify the purpose, define the goal, identify stakeholders, and assess feasibility.
2 · Planning
Set scope, schedule, budget, responsibilities, quality standards, and risk strategy.
3 · Execution
Perform the work, coordinate the team, and produce deliverables.
4 · Monitoring & Control
Track progress, compare to the plan, manage changes, and address issues before they grow.
5 · Closing
Confirm acceptance, transfer handover, record lessons learned, and formally complete the project.

7 Major Responsibilities of a PM

Define objectives · Plan the project · Organize the team · Manage time · Manage resources · Manage risks · Communicate. The PM is the key link between the technical team and the business stakeholders.
The Project Manager
The PM acts as the coordinator, planner, communicator, and decision-support leader. They do not necessarily do all tasks themselves, but they ensure the right people do the right work at the right time, with the right information.

Six Golden Rules of PM

Embrace the Temporary · Balance the Scale (scope, time, cost, quality, resources, and risk are linked) · Involve stakeholders continuously · Anticipate risk · Control change · Communicate constantly.
📘 Lesson 2 — Project Management & the IT Context

A Systems View of Project Management

System
A group of interconnected components that work together to achieve a common goal. A project is part of a larger system of people, processes, technology, and objectives.
Systems View of PM
This approach sees the project as part of a larger organizational environment, not as isolated work. The project is affected by business direction, team culture, process design, and technology constraints.
Systems Philosophy
An organization and its projects are not separate islands; they are connected systems with shared goals and interdependent parts.
Systems Analysis
The process of examining a system’s parts, problems, requirements, relationships, and solutions before making a change.
Systems Management
Aligning business, organizational, and technical factors so the system achieves its intended purpose and value.

Three-Sphere Model

Business
Strategy, market need, value, and organizational goals.
Organizational
People, culture, structure, policies, leadership, and operating processes.
Technology
Hardware, software, networks, data, infrastructure, and support systems.
Why it matters
A project may be technically brilliant but still fail if the business case is weak, the organization resists change, or the system does not fit user needs.

Organizations & Structures

Organization
A structured group of people working together by roles, processes, and shared responsibilities.
Organizational Structure
The arrangement of authority, reporting lines, communication, and responsibility across the organization.
Functional
Employees are grouped by department or specialization.
Projectized
Employees are assigned to projects and the PM has significant authority.
Matrix
Employees report to both a functional manager and a project manager.
Weak Matrix
Functional manager has more authority than the PM.
Balanced Matrix
Authority is shared between the functional manager and the PM.
Strong Matrix
The PM has greater authority and resource control.
Organizational Culture
Shared values, beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors that influence how work gets done and how change is accepted.
Organizational Change
A shift in technology, structure, processes, policies, or employee roles that changes how work is performed.
Resistance to Change
Reluctance or pushback when a new process, technology, or system is introduced.
Project Governance
The rules, responsibilities, oversight, and decision-making framework that guide project direction and control.

Stakeholder Management

Stakeholder
Any person, group, or organization that can affect, be affected by, or perceive itself to be affected by a project.
Internal
Employees, managers, project team members, and internal departments.
External
Customers, vendors, consultants, regulators, and other outside groups.
Stakeholder Register
A master list of stakeholders with their roles, interests, expectations, influence, and communication needs.
Stakeholder Analysis
Examines stakeholders by power, interest, influence, needs, and potential impact on the project.
Power–Interest Grid
A tool that sorts stakeholders according to their authority and interest in the project.
Stakeholder Engagement Plan
Describes how each stakeholder group will be informed, consulted, involved, or managed throughout the project.
Stakeholder Management Process
Identify stakeholders → Analyze their power, interest, and impact → Engage and Communicate with them → Monitor changes in their needs and influence.

Power–Interest strategies

Project Sponsor
Provides resources, high-level direction, and ownership support.
Project Team
Performs the work required to achieve project outcomes.
End User
Uses the final product or service; their acceptance is critical.

Exam tip
Stakeholder success is not just about satisfying the client. It is about managing expectations, reducing resistance, and keeping key people engaged throughout delivery.

Context of IT Projects

IT Project
A temporary effort to create, improve, implement, or maintain a technology-based product, service, or information system.
Scope Creep
Uncontrolled additions to the project’s requirements without proper approval or adjustment.
Project Constraint
A limiting factor such as scope, time, cost, quality, resources, or risk.
Project Risk
An uncertain event or condition that can positively or negatively affect project objectives.
System Integration
Connecting different applications, databases, platforms, and devices so they work as one system.
User Acceptance
The willingness of the intended users to accept, adopt, and properly use the system.
Cybersecurity
Protecting systems, networks, data, and applications from unauthorized use or attacks.
Data Privacy
The safe handling of personal or sensitive information through collection, storage, use, and protection policies.
Technical Feasibility
Whether the technology, skills, infrastructure, and resources are sufficient to build the project.
Operational Feasibility
Whether the solution can work effectively within existing processes, users, and operations.
DevOps
A practice that unifies development and IT operations to deliver software faster and more reliably.
DevSecOps
A modern approach that integrates security into the development and operations lifecycle from the start.
Cloud Computing
The delivery of computing resources such as storage, servers, databases, and software over the internet.
Artificial Intelligence
Systems that perform tasks requiring human-like reasoning, learning, or decision-making.
Innovation
Creating or adopting new ideas, technologies, methods, or solutions to improve outcomes.
Final takeaway
IT projects are not only technical tasks. They are business initiatives shaped by stakeholder needs, organizational realities, risk, quality, and the practical ability to deliver value in a changing digital environment.
